Sudden cardiac death prediction and prevention

We want to learn whether our electrocardiogram (ECG) model identifies people at higher risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in new populations, how it predicts other outcomes related to SCD, and what these predictions can teach us about the underlying anatomy and physiology of SCD.

This page covers data preparation, model access, computing needs, ethics approvals, and possible prospective studies. To request model access, send us the access form (you can also just send us an email with those terms if you prefer), along with your GitHub username. The model repository is private. We grant read access after reviewing your agreement and GitHub username.

1 Data: sample and outcomes

Sample. It would be nice to get an approximate sense of the sample you have available:

  • Population, how they were sampled (inclusion criteria), years covered

  • Numbers of people and ECGs, and the age and sex mix

Design. Ideally, for population-based outcomes (e.g., death certificates, diagnosis codes, etc.) we’d use a full population based sample: for example, all patients with ECGs collected over a fixed time period in a population or health system. For more focused studies, we can use the subset of patients who had a diagnostic test (e.g., all patients who had a cardiac MRI or an endomyocardial biopsy, linked to their ECGs) or patients with a defibrillator (e.g., appropriate shocks), accounting for the fact that this is a very selected sample and that estimates will not generalize to the full population. Case series or case-control designs are also possible, if it’s a particularly interesting question where a full census of some population is not feasible (e.g., investigating ECG-predicted risk in a small number of young athletes who experienced SCD).

Our analysis happens at the level of the ECG, and accounts for repeated recordings from the same patient when calculating uncertainty (clustering by patient), as in the original paper.

Outcomes. No definition of SCD is perfect, and each dataset has its own advantages and disadvantages. We’ll choose the main outcome your records can support: e.g., death-certificate SCD, death from any cause, ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation, cardiac arrest, or defibrillator therapy. We need to establish:

  • Definitions and event dates available in clinical records, registries or autopsy findings

  • Follow-up from each ECG, including when it ends, how events outside the hospital are captured, and how to handle missing follow-up

We’ll use 12-month outcomes where possible. Other analyses could cover different time periods, changes across repeated ECGs, imaging and physiological measurements.

The published Swedish SCD definition combines one qualifying cause below and the location/timing rule.

Definition component Published rule
Heart disease cause ICD-10 I00–I09, I11, or I20–I51
Congenital heart disease cause ICD-10 Q20–Q24
Ill-defined cause ICD-10 R95–R99
Location and timing Death outside hospital or within the first 24 hours of hospital admission

The definition comes from Nature supplementary information, section VII.B.

2 Data: ECGs

ECGs can be in any format - XML, EDF, CSV, PDF or scans. If we need to do conversion, we need the recording system, lead labels, units, recording length and whether the leads were recorded at the same time. It’s pretty easy to convert the ECGs into the model’s input format and prepare a linked clinical CSV with clearly explained columns.

The model takes as input a numerical array with 12 rows × 5,000 samples: ten seconds at 500 measurements per second (500 Hz), in millivolts. The lead order is I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, V1–V6. It uses waveforms alone; the reference reader loads NumPy .npy files. Conversion needs to account for sampling rate, units, lead order and any recorded −aVR sign. For PDFs, scans or short recordings, we’ll check which signals and time segments are present and assess any digitization or reconstruction needed. If needed, for shorter recordings (e.g., 2.5 seconds per lead) we can retrain our model or use median beats.

3 Model

Access. It’s easy to give your team access to the model. The only thing we need is a record of who is using the model and a signed form or written assent to responsible research use. Note this doesn’t need to be an institutional agreement - we’re happy with you as an individual committing to noncommercial use, keeping the model secure, and limits on sharing and reuse. The blank research agreement is the easiest way to do this, but whatever you’re comfortable with (e.g., an email) is also fine. Each person using the model agrees to these terms. Once we’ve filed your completed form or email assent to these terms and have your GitHub usernames, we’ll provide read access to the private model repository.

We’ll use the private model repository for model files and execution code. We’ll share only approved summary results outside your data environment. Model-guided recruitment, return of results or clinical use requires separate written authorization.

Computing. We’d prefer if the ECGs never leave your health system infrastructure: it’s much easier to get the model in than to get data out. So we’d like to know anything you can share about your computing environment - where the ECGs live or where they can be placed within the health system infrastructure: CPU or GPU, memory and storage, and whether you’ll use local or cloud computing. Options include your existing workstation or server, an institution-approved virtual machine, or an approved cloud environment. We’ll test a small batch to check that the model runs correctly and establish memory needs and running time.

Checks. The first step is to make sure the model is reading the ECGs correctly by performing a few checks:

  • Signal quality, lead identity and direction, and units

  • Record matching and sample counts

  • Reference predictions, score distributions and repeatability

We can compare sex predictions with Swedish benchmarks supplied by Alex and add age prediction if a compatible model is available. Further analyses could examine aVL features using the public implementation.

4 IRB and regulatory requirements

For a retrospective study of routinely-collected data, IRB approval is required but usually quite straightforward, since patient records, ECGs, identifiers and individual predictions will stay in the approved environment (the only thing that leaves is approved summary results and code). We can help adapt our existing study materials for your local ethics committee or institutional review board (IRB).

5 Later: Prospective study and AI clinic

Prospective study. A starting design would invite higher- and lower-risk participants for the same baseline assessment, 14-day ECG monitoring and cardiac ultrasound, with two-year follow-up through clinical and mortality records. We need to agree:

  • Main rhythm outcome, sample size and event review

  • Communication of important findings

  • Staffing and test costs

The US protocol draft and Sweden protocol draft provide more detailed examples.

AI clinic. After we assess the model in local data, we can explore a pathway in which people with high scores are offered a cardiology consultation under the agreed clinical and research approvals. The clinician investigates the result using history, ECGs, monitoring, imaging and other tests as appropriate. The plan needs to cover:

  • Referrals and clinician training

  • Consent and return of results

  • Responsibility for care and follow-up

  • Payment for visits and tests
